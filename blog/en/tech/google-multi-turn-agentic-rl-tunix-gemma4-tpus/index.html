Google Advances Agentic AI with Multi-Turn Reinforcement Learning on TPUs

Google's new method for training multi-turn, tool-using LLM agents with Tunix and Gemma 4 on Cloud TPUs promises significant improvements in complex reasoning and tool compliance, pushing the boundaries of agentic AI.

Published November 20, 20265 min readAI and Models
Image source: Google

Confirmed facts

Google Developers announced a new method for training multi-turn, tool-using Large Language Model (LLM) agents. This method leverages Tunix and Gemma 4, and its training is conducted on Google Cloud TPUs [1].

The core algorithmic approach is Agentic Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). This is complemented by the use of asynchronous vLLM rollouts [1].

The method is designed to enhance agents' capabilities in complex reasoning and tool compliance within interactive environments [1].

Tunix is described as a reinforcement learning framework specifically engineered for agentic LLMs [1]. Gemma 4 is utilized as the LLM, identified as the latest generation of Google's open models [1].

GRPO functions by optimizing agent policies through comparing the performance of a group of agents against a defined baseline. This mechanism is intended to foster exploration while simultaneously maintaining training stability [1].

Asynchronous vLLM rollouts are employed to generate high-throughput and diverse interaction data, which is crucial for the training process [1].

The method directly addresses challenges such as long-horizon planning and the propagation of errors across multiple interaction turns [1].

Practical outcomes include a reported 25% improvement in task completion rate on the ToolUse-Complex benchmark compared to previous methods [1].

The deployment on Cloud TPUs facilitates scalable and efficient training for these computationally intensive reinforcement learning tasks [1].

The approach enables agents to learn from their own errors and refine their operational strategies over successive interaction turns [1].

"Tool compliance" is defined as the agent's capacity to accurately identify, invoke, and utilize external tools as necessitated by a given task [1]. "Complex reasoning" refers to the agent's ability to decompose multi-step problems, manage internal state, and adapt to dynamic environmental changes [1].

Google has provided code examples and a walkthrough to assist developers in implementing this training method [1].

Technical analysis

Developing AI agents capable of performing complex, multi-step tasks in dynamic environments presents significant engineering challenges. Traditional LLM applications often operate in single-turn query-response cycles. However, real-world scenarios frequently demand agents that can engage in extended interactions, maintain context, utilize external tools, and adapt their strategy over multiple turns. The confirmed challenges of "long-horizon planning" and "error propagation" [1] highlight the difficulty in ensuring an agent's actions remain coherent and effective across a sequence of operations, where an early mistake can derail an entire task. This necessitates robust learning mechanisms that can handle sequential decision-making and feedback loops.

The integration of Tunix, a reinforcement learning framework specifically designed for agentic LLMs, with Gemma 4, Google's latest open model, represents a targeted architectural choice [1]. Tunix's specialization for agentic LLMs implies it provides abstractions and optimizations relevant to the unique requirements of training agents, such as managing tool invocation, parsing observations, and structuring multi-turn dialogues. The use of Gemma 4 as the underlying LLM suggests leveraging its generative and reasoning capabilities as the agent's "brain." This combination allows the LLM to generate actions and interpret observations, while Tunix orchestrates the learning process based on environmental feedback. The "open model" aspect of Gemma 4 could facilitate broader adoption and experimentation by developers, though specific licensing or deployment details were not disclosed [1].

The adoption of Agentic Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is central to the method's ability to improve agent performance. GRPO's mechanism of comparing a group of agents against a baseline to optimize policies [1] suggests an approach that balances exploration (trying new strategies) with stability (avoiding catastrophic forgetting or divergence). This is particularly valuable in complex, interactive environments where the optimal policy might not be immediately obvious, and agents need to discover effective sequences of actions. The "agentic" prefix implies GRPO has been adapted to the specific context of LLM agents, likely incorporating mechanisms for handling discrete actions (like tool calls) and structured observations.

The complementary use of asynchronous vLLM rollouts directly addresses the data efficiency challenge inherent in reinforcement learning. RL algorithms typically require vast amounts of interaction data to learn effective policies. By generating "high-throughput, diverse interaction data" asynchronously [1], the system can rapidly collect experiences from the environment, accelerating the training loop. This parallelization of data collection is critical for scaling RL to complex tasks and large models, reducing the wall-clock time required for training and making the process more feasible for developers.

For software engineers, this method offers a concrete pathway to developing more capable and reliable AI agents. The reported 25% improvement in task completion rate on the ToolUse-Complex benchmark [1] is a significant practical outcome, indicating that agents trained with this approach are more effective at solving real-world problems requiring tool use and complex reasoning. Developers can leverage the provided code examples and walkthrough [1] to implement similar training pipelines, potentially reducing the barrier to entry for advanced agent development.

The ability of agents to "learn from their own mistakes and refine their strategies over multiple interaction turns" [1] implies a higher degree of autonomy and robustness. This reduces the need for extensive manual rule-setting or prompt engineering for every possible scenario, shifting the development paradigm towards defining environments and reward functions. The emphasis on "tool compliance" and "complex reasoning" directly translates to agents that are more dependable in automating workflows, interacting with APIs, or navigating digital environments where precise action and logical progression are paramount.

The reliance on Google Cloud TPUs for training underscores the computational intensity of this advanced RL approach [1]. TPUs are specialized hardware accelerators optimized for machine learning workloads, particularly matrix multiplications common in neural network training. Their use enables "scalable and efficient training" [1], which is crucial for iterating on large LLM agents and complex environments. For developers, this means that while the method is powerful, access to scalable, specialized hardware like TPUs is a practical consideration for implementing and experimenting with it at scale. The provision of a walkthrough suggests Google aims to make this powerful capability accessible to developers within their cloud ecosystem.

While the announcement details the methodology and its benefits, specific architectural details of Tunix beyond its description as an RL framework for agentic LLMs were not disclosed. Similarly, the precise internal architecture or training specifics of Gemma 4 were not provided, beyond its identification as Google's latest open model [1]. Developers evaluating this approach would need to consider the computational resources required, the effort involved in defining appropriate interactive environments, and the design of effective reward signals for their specific agent tasks. The provided code examples offer a starting point for such evaluations, allowing engineers to assess the method's applicability and performance in their own contexts.
